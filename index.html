A short history of our area

Alpine Heights is a private estate just off Bawnogue Road, about a kilometre from Clondalkin village. The ground it stands on has a much longer story, from an early Christian monastery and Viking raids to the farmland that became one of Dublin's newest suburbs.

  1. c. 600 AD

    A monastery on the Camac

    Clondalkin takes its name from the Irish Cluain Dolcáin, “Dolcán's meadow”. Saint Cronan Mochua is believed to have founded a monastery beside the River Camac around the late 6th or early 7th century. Its round tower still stands in the village today.

  2. 832–1014

    The Viking years

    Vikings sacked Clondalkin in 832 and burned the monastery. Later in the 9th century, Amlaíb, a Norse king of Dublin, built a fortress here, which Irish forces burned in 867. The district stayed under Norse influence until Brian Boru's victory at Clontarf in 1014.

  3. 1171

    The last High King

    Ruaidrí Ua Conchobair, the last High King of Ireland, camped at Clondalkin while trying to retake Dublin from Strongbow's Norman forces.

  4. 1800s

    Bawnoges: the “small green field”

    Bawnogue takes its name from the old townland of Bawnoges, in Irish Bánóg, a word the 1830s Ordnance Survey glossed as “small green field”. It was a farming townland of about 29 acres in the civil parish of Clondalkin and the barony of Uppercross, next to Clonburris Great, Deansrath and Fairview.

  5. 1946–1981

    From village to suburb

    As Dublin grew westward, Clondalkin changed fast. Its population went from about 1,000 in 1946 to over 23,000 by 1981, and the fields around Bawnogue became new housing estates.

  6. 1977–1980

    A parish of our own

    Bawnogue became its own parish, separate from Clondalkin, in 1977. The foundation stone of the Church of the Transfiguration was blessed by Pope John Paul II at Maynooth during his 1979 visit and laid in March 1980. The Spiritan Fathers have served the parish since 1988.
